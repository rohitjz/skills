# The PM Writing System

One place for how to write well as a PM. The voice stays constant; only a few dials flex by reader. Distilled from the writing canon (Zinsser, Strunk & White, Orwell, Ogilvy, Scott Adams, Zweig, Dillard), McKinsey's Minto, Amazon's memo culture, and the Finshots house style for explaining hard things simply.

Work the passes in order: know the reader and the one point, draft fast, cut hard, then run the craft and the send checks.

## Make it yours

This is an opinionated default, not scripture. The craft (drafting, cutting, sentence moves, framing) is universal. The voice rules below are one writer's taste; keep what fits you and change what doesn't. Start by writing your own voice in one line.

## Voice (constant)

Define your voice in a single line and never break it. A strong default: direct, concrete, opinionated when it matters, never performative.

The test: if you removed your name, would a teammate still know you wrote it?

## Non-negotiables

1. **No em-dashes.** Use commas, periods, semicolons, colons, or parentheses.
2. **No AI vocabulary.** Never: delve, crucial, landscape, tapestry, foster, underscore, showcase, leverage, pivotal, paradigm.
3. **No filler.** Cut "it's important to note," "it goes without saying," "at the end of the day."
4. **No sycophancy or significance inflation.** Nothing is "groundbreaking" unless it literally is.
5. **Rule of three: earned, not reflexive.** Don't pad a list to three because three sounds complete ("we listen, we learn, we adapt"). A deliberate, load-bearing triad is a real persuasion device, so use it on purpose. Test: could you defend dropping one of the three? If not, the three are earned.
6. **No negative parallelisms.** Avoid "not just X, it's Y."
7. **Concrete over vague.** "Retention dropped 4% in Q3" beats "we're seeing challenges."
8. **Have a point of view.** Neutral is boring. Recommend, don't summarize options.
9. **Vary sentence rhythm.** Short sentences land. Then a longer one to carry the argument. Then short again.
10. **Precise and concise, strictly.** Every word fights for its place.
11. **The read-aloud test.** If it sounds weird spoken, rewrite it.

## Before you write

Answer three questions (McKinsey Pyramid):
1. Who is reading, and what should they decide or do after?
2. What is my single main point? If you can't say it in one sentence, you haven't thought enough. Leave the reader one provocative thought, not five.
3. What is the minimum evidence this reader needs to accept it?

## Draft: generate first, edit second

Composing and editing are different jobs. Don't do both at once.
- Pop the cork: once you start, keep pouring; don't revise what you've already written on the first pass.
- Talk it onto the page, then clean it up.
- Stuck? Say out loud "I want you to know about this because..." and write exactly that.
- When a sentence jams, cut it or restart it, then move on.
- Don't be a prisoner of the outline.

## Cut: the one-third rule

Concision is a process, not a wish.
- A first draft usually cuts by half without losing meaning or voice. If you can't cut at least a third, it isn't worth reading.
- Draft long, cut hard, rest a day, then a final pass.
- Detach your ego: read it as a stranger's work (paste it into a different font). Whatever you like most is often what readers like least.
- Tweak a draft that is basically right; restart one that "sounds like writing."
- Ogilvy's caps: never more than two pages on any subject. If what you want is action, sometimes don't write at all, go tell the person.

## Sentence moves

| Move | Do this |
|---|---|
| **End-stress** | Put the emphatic words at the end. "Since that time, humanity has advanced in many ways, but it has hardly advanced in fortitude." |
| **Sequence** | After every sentence, ask what the reader wants to know next, then answer that. |
| **Kill adverbs** | A weak adverb hides a weak verb. "Grinned widely" wants a better verb. Cut reflexive adjectives too. |
| **Precise verbs** | "Resigned," "retired," or "was fired," not "stepped down." |
| **Personal nouns** | "Most people just laugh with disbelief," not "the common reaction is incredulous laughter." |
| **But / however** | Open with "But" if you like. Never open with "however"; it hangs there like a wet dishrag. |
| **That vs. which** | Use "that" unless it makes the meaning ambiguous. If the clause needs a comma, it probably needs "which." |

## Openings and endings

- The lead is the most important sentence you write. If it doesn't pull the reader into the second sentence, the piece is dead. Rewrite it until it does. Keep it fresh: "X was founded in..." is not a lead.
- The last line deserves as much thought as the first. Don't summarize, and never write "In conclusion." End on a line that jolts and yet feels inevitable, or bring the lead full circle.

## Memorable framing

- Earned triads (see non-negotiable 5).
- Reach for a structure the reader already trusts (SCQA, the Pyramid, the Iceberg, a popular mental model) instead of scaffolding they have to learn.
- Reduce abstraction to a picture: turn the number or the principle into something they can see (NASA's mission costs less than Americans spend on lip balm).
- Write from depth (Hemingway's iceberg): show one-eighth, know all eight-eighths. The precision of what you say signals the depth of what you left out.

## Professional humor

Intelligent surprise, never cringe or pomp.
- Humor is built on surprise. Don't strain for laughs; you can only surprise a reader so often.
- Stay in control; make yourself the dunce only to a point.
- The lever is word choice. "Swill," not "drink."
- Guardrail: if a line would read as trying too hard or pompous quoted back to you, cut it. Humor serves the point, never replaces it, and never punches down.

## Explaining and teaching: the Finshots toolkit

For learning guides, newsletters, explainers, and external education, write the way the Finshots newsletter does. This conversational register is for teaching, not for exec memos.

- **Anchor then pivot.** Open in something the reader has lived or already knows (second person), then flip to the surprise in one line.
- **Use "you."** Put the reader inside the story.
- **Define on first use.** Every term of art gets a one-line plain gloss, often flagged: "In simple terms, hedging is...". Assume the reader knows nothing.
- **One analogy per hard idea.** "Inventing a new drug is a little like buying lottery tickets that cost millions of dollars each."
- **Voice the reader's question, then answer it** ("But what else could they do?"). Use sparingly so it stays sharp.
- **The Explainer Arc:** anchor, tension, "here's the catch," steelman both sides, land on an open question. A story spine with soft bold signposts, not a bulleted memo. Refuse the tidy summary; end on the stakes.
- **Digest format** (weekly roundup): modular labeled segments, each a compressed mini-arc, reader involvement, a warm recurring sign-off.
- **A time budget, not a word count.** Promise a short read and keep it.

## Adjust for the reader (lightly)

The voice doesn't change. Only a few dials do:
- **Leadership:** shorter, lead with the ask, every sentence earns its place, data with a clear "what I need and by when." SCQA to open, Pyramid for the body.
- **Team:** warmer, an invitation not an order, start from a shared observation.
- **Teaching or external:** use the explainer toolkit above; lead with what changes for the reader.

## Before you send

Authenticity checks:
1. Does this match what I will actually do?
2. Am I being specific, or hiding behind abstract language?
3. Would I write this differently if it were quoted back in six months?
4. Is any part performative? Cut it.
5. Does the tone match the relationship?

Timing (Kairos): what just happened, when will they read it, what is their emotional state?

## Frameworks quick-reference

| Framework | Use |
|---|---|
| SCQA (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer) | Opening exec or peer comms |
| Pyramid Principle | Body: answer first, then grouped evidence |
| What, So What, Now What | Quick emails, feedback, messages |
| The Explainer Arc | Teaching: anchor, tension, catch, both sides, open question |
| Iceberg Theory | Write from depth; show one-eighth, know eight-eighths |
| The One-Third Cut | If you can't cut a draft by a third, you haven't edited it |
| End-Stress | Put the emphatic words at the end |
| Orwell's Six Rules | Editing pass: clichés, short words, cut, active voice |
| Ogilvy's "How to Write" memo | Memos: two-page cap, crystal-clear action gate, read aloud next morning |
| Cognitive Fluency | Simpler words read as more credible |
| Kairos | Check timing before sending |
